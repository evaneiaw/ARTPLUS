- Printing too much ink on a page will cause oversaturation: a large quantity of ink will build up, which will dry slowly and the oversaturated part of the paper is more likely to stretch, warp, wrinkle or tear.
- Using unnecessary ink adds unnecessary cost to a printing job.

Depending on the type of images you are dealing with, different compression methods are advised. Using the appropriate compression methods where necessary can significantly reduce PDF file sizes.
The JPEG compression method is best suited for grayscale or color bitmap images. A typical example of an image for which JPEG compression is advised is a continuous-tone photograph. This type of image contains more information, more details, than can be reproduced on screen or in print. Therefore, it makes sense to remove the information that cannot be displayed anyway. This can result in a loss of image quality, as the compression method removes information: it is called a lossy reduction method. However, file sizes are greatly reduced by JPEG compression.
The ZIP compression method is best suited for vector graphics (line art), images that contain large areas of a single color or repeating patterns. Typical examples of this kind of images include screen shots and simple graphics created in MS Paint. ZIP compression is lossless, which means that no information is removed from the image during compression.
Detect images that were not compressed during the conversion of the document to PDF form.
The JBIG2 compression method is best suited for 1-bit images, but it can also be used for coding grayscale and color images with limited numbers of bits per pixel. Depending on the setting used by the producer of the PDF, JBIG2 can be a lossy compression method, which means that some information is removed from the images during compression.
The CCITT compression method is best suited for 1-bit images made by paint programs, as well as for images scanned with an image depth of 1 bit. CCITT is a lossless compression method, which means that no information is removed from the images during compression.
The LZW compression discovers and exploits many patterns in images or text (for example by using a differencing technique). In general high compression rates can be achieved with this compression algorithm. 
The RunLength compression method is best suited for images that contain large areas of solid white or black. It is a lossless compression method, which means that no information is removed from the images during compression.

Spot colors are printed with special premixed inks, used instead of, or in addition to, CMYK inks (process colors). Spot color printing requires a separate plate for every spot color, whereas process color printing only uses four plates, one for each of the four basic CMYK colors. Spot colors are especially well suited for offset printing, but should be used with caution when creating documents for digital output or monitor display. Note that spot colors are also more expensive to print.

Downsample (decrease the resolution of) the image to a specific resolution. When an image is downsampled, the color (value) of each of the new, larger dots or areas needs to be determined. Several methods of calculation are available:
- Subsampling: in this type of downsampling, the new dots assume the value of the dot that was at the center of the corresponding area in the original image.
- Average downsampling: in this type of downsampling, each new dot assumes the calculated average value of the corresponding group of dots in the original image.
- Bicubic downsampling: in this type of downsampling, the value of the new dots is calculated on the basis of a weighted average of the values of the corresponding group of dots in the original image.

This checks ink coverage of pages or objects, using one of the following methods:
- A full ink coverage check of the page, where all objects are taken into account, honoring overprint, transparency, layers, ... The check can be limited to the area inside the page box of your choice.
- A basic object check that inspects the total amount of ink of a basic vector object on its own, ignoring overlap, overprint, transparency, masking, ... It is an option to check images as well. The check can be limited to the objects in the page box of your choice.
It is possible to ignore small areas that exceed the allowed ink coverage. The area size you can specify for this, is the edge size of the smallest square area in which the average ink coverage has to exceed the limit before reporting an error or warning. When entering "0", even one dot exceeding the allowed ink coverage will be reported.
To avoid some problems it is important to limit the total ink coverage. In theory, for example, you could create black by combining 100% of cyan, magenta and yellow. In practice, however, it is recommended not to create process colors with high ink contents for three reasons:
- Ink pigments are by nature imperfect and, consequently, the resulting colors might not be sharp. Black created from 100 % cyan, magenta and yellow, for example, will be muddy brown.
- Printing too much ink on a page will cause oversaturation: a large quantity of ink will build up, which will dry slowly and the oversaturated part of the paper is more likely to stretch, warp, wrinkle or tear.
- Using unnecessary ink adds unnecessary cost to a printing job.

Change stroke adjustment takes care of the rendering of paths on a raster device (printing device, computer monitor, etc.). If the stroke adjustment is put on 'off', the device will draw the pixel if it covers the path for more than 50%. This means that a line from 1 pixel width can be drawn in different ways:
- if the pixel is drawn nicely on top of a pixel of the screen, the line will look fine.
- if the pixel is drawn over 2 pixels of the screen, that means the line will be 2 pixels on some places and 1 pixel on other places, turning the line into an ugly, jaggy line.
The stroke adjustment will provide a correction when rendering to make look the line 1 pixel width everywhere.
